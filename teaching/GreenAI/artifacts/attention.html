Attention, Step by Step

How a translation model decides which source words to look at while it writes each output word. The running example is the start of our sentence: Unha tía miña, que todo o solucionaba con ditos e refráns, dicía algo así como «un é escravo do que di e dono do que cala». It continues the neural translation page, which shows how an encoder and a decoder translate without attention.

1Attention: look back at every word

A translation model has an encoder, which reads the Galician words, and a decoder, which writes the English words one at a time. Each box below is the encoder's summary after one more word, as on the translation page. Without attention, the decoder gets only the last box, so it uses the words in the proportions that box happens to hold: mostly the last ones. With attention, it can look back at every box, and it chooses new proportions for every word it writes.

2Two trained models: without and with attention

Two small translation models, trained on the same data for the same number of steps, with the same number of parameters. The only difference: without attention, the decoder gets the encoder's last vector, the summary of the whole sentence; with attention, it looks back at every word's vector each time it writes a word. The training data is built from templates around our sentence (other relatives, other verbs, other sayings). Our own sentence was left out, so both models translate it for the first time.

Real sentences hold much more than these templates: with 64 dimensions, one vector holds this toy data perfectly, but real models of 2014 lost details in long sentences (Cho et al. 2014; Bahdanau et al. 2015). Attention solved this without making the vector bigger.

3Query, key, score, softmax, value

Each source word offers a key: what it has. The word being written asks with a query: what it is looking for. The score is the dot product of query and key; softmax turns the scores into weights that sum to 1; the output is the weighted average of the source words' values. Drag the orange query.

5Self-attention, and many heads at once

The encoder uses the same mechanism inside one sentence: every Galician word attends to every Galician word, itself included. Each head has its own queries and keys, so each head learns its own pattern. The heads' outputs are then combined into one vector per word.

Pick a head, then click a word.

6Who decides what a head does?

Nobody. The people who build a model choose how many layers and heads it has, how big they are, and what it is trained to predict. They do not assign jobs to heads. Every head starts as random numbers, and training nudges all of them at once in whatever direction lowers the error. A head ends up with a job because, from its own random start, doing that job was the quickest way down. Researchers find the jobs afterwards, by looking, as we do here.

Below, a tiny model with 2 layers of 2 heads learns the classic sentence The animal didn't cross the street because it was tired: it has to say what “it” refers to. The adjective decides: tired means the animal, wide means the street. It trains on 96 such sentences. The three runs differ only in their random start.
