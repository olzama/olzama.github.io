Neural Translation, Step by Step

How a neural model translates without attention: where its vectors come from, how the encoder reads a Galician sentence into one summary, and how the decoder writes English from it. The running example is the start of a sentence from the novel Speechwriter: Unha tía miña, que todo o solucionaba con ditos e refráns, dicía algo así como «un é escravo do que di e dono do que cala».

1Without attention: where a translation model's vectors come from

A neural translation model has two parts. The encoder reads the Galician words into a summary, a vector; the decoder starts from that summary and writes the English words one at a time. Every word in this model is a 2-D vector in one of three tables, and all of them start random: before it can translate, the model has to learn them from examples, by training. Here the 16 pairs are taken one at a time, round after round, and after every pair the tables are updated a little. Section 2 shows the encoder's rule with simple made-up numbers; section 3 takes the trained encoder and decoder apart.

2Without attention: the encoder reads a sentence into one vector

A translation model starts by reading the Galician sentence. Each word has an embedding, a row in a table like p on the skip-gram page; here it has 2 dimensions, so every word is a point. The encoder reads the words in order and keeps a summary: one more vector, updated after every word. In this simplified encoder, each new word moves the summary halfway toward that word's point.

The word vectors are made up for this example. Real encoders, recurrent neural networks, learn their update rule instead of always taking half and half, and they can learn to keep some things longer. But their summary is still one vector of a fixed size, whatever the length of the sentence.

3Without attention: from Galician to English, word by word

The model trained in section 1, at work. The encoder reads the phrase into a summary, and Galician and English words share one plane. The decoder then writes English one word at a time. It keeps a state, a vector, and its first state is the summary. At every step it chooses a word the way skip-gram predicted neighbors, then folds that word into its state with the same ½ + ½ rule the encoder used.

c. If the summary is mostly the end of the phrase, how does the first word come out right?

summary = ⅛ unha + ¼ tía + ½ miña, so every English word's score at position 1 = the part from ⅛ unha + the part from ¼ tía + ½ miña:

Without the part from unha, ‹end› would score highest: the model would write nothing. With it, “an” wins.

In long sentences the first words fade much further: in section 2's 35-piece sentence, tía is 0.534 of the summary. Sutskever and colleagues (2014) fed the sentence in reverse, so its first words are freshest when the decoder starts; their BLEU score (0–100, overlap with translations by people) rose from 25.9 to 30.6. Attention removes the need for the trick: the decoder looks back at every word (attention page).

d. Is “an aunt of mine” the best translation?

The toy model learned to write “an aunt of mine” for unha tía miña, and the real model writes the same (section 5). It is correct and faithful: “an” keeps the new character that unha introduces. But English rarely says it. For every 100 “my …”, English books write “a … of mine” this often (log scale):

a friend of mine12.47
an aunt of mine0.36
a brother of mine0.08

“A … of mine” picks one out of several, which is natural for friends and marked for close family. A translator would rebuild the sentence: “I had an aunt who…”. No word of the Galician corresponds to “I had”, and our model never saw such a sentence in training, so it cannot write it. Section 4 trains a model that does. Counts from Google Books; the n-gram page, section 7, has the full chart and how it was counted.

4Without attention: when the training data says “I had an aunt who…”

The decoder can write any English word it knows, also words that have no Galician counterpart, such as “I had”. What it writes comes from its training data. This second small model is trained on sentences whose English follows the book counts: “a friend of mine” and “a colleague of mine” stay, but “an aunt of mine” and “a brother of mine” never appear. For aunts and brothers, the English is rebuilt the way a translator would.

5Why do real translation models still write “an aunt of mine”?

The real Galician–English model, Helsinki-NLP/opus-mt-gl-en, translates our sentence as “An aunt of mine, who…”, even though English books rarely say it. We can ask the model how likely it finds other openings: give it the Galician sentence and an English opening, and read off its probability for each word.

The model's probability for each opening (log scale)

“An aunt of mine” is the model's favorite by far; “I had an aunt” is tens of thousands of times less likely. Likely reasons:

  • It learned translations, not English in general. Book counts show how often English writers choose a phrasing. A translation model learns how translators rendered similar Galician phrases. Unha X miña is usually rendered “a X of mine”, which is right for friends and colleagues, and the model applies the same pattern to tía.
  • Rare is not absent. “An aunt of mine” is unusual, but the pattern “a … of mine” is very common, so the model writes it fluently.
  • Rebuilding a sentence is a big step. “I had an aunt who…” needs a new subject and verb. The model chooses one word at a time, given the Galician, and staying close to the Galician wins at each step.
  • Galician is low-resource. There is relatively little translated Galician text to learn from, and much of it is fairly literal.

These are likely explanations, not measured ones. The toy model of section 4 shows the first point directly: what the training data says is what the model writes.
