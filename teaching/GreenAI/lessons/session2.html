Weights are numbers

How the weights produce an answer

  • Qwen2.5-1.5B with its trained weights, and the same model with random weights

A model is a collection of numbers

A computer stores every number as bits

Binary: the same place values as decimal, with powers of 2

  • A bit is 0 or 1
  • Each position is worth twice the one to its right: 1, 2, 4, 8, …
  • A model's parameters are numbers: billions of them, all stored as bits

Integers

Can integers store the weights?

A floating-point number has three parts

The IEEE 754 standard: sign, exponent, mantissa

value = sign × (1 + mantissa) × 2exponent − bias

  • FP16: a 16-bit floating-point number. FP32 uses 32 bits.
  • The next slides show where the 1 and the bias in this formula come from

Moving the point

  • The place values continue past the point: each position is worth half the one to its left. The same happens in decimal: 13 = 1.3 × 10¹

Precision is a number of digits

Formats side by side

  • A scale of sizes: each gridline is × 10¹⁰. 1 is in the middle, and 0 is not on it. Each float format stores the same sizes with a minus sign too
  • Mantissa bits buy precision: about 7 digits in FP32, 3 in FP16, 2 in BF16

Complete weight_memory_gb: memory in gigabytes = parameters × bits ÷ 8, divided by 109 bytes per GB. Then the loop checks 7B, 32B and 70B models in FP16, INT8 and INT4 against the 16 GB of a Colab T4. Ignore everything except the weights.

Hints

  • Bits ÷ 8 gives bytes; bytes ÷ 1e9 gives gigabytes.
  • A model fits if its weights take less than the GPU's memory.

When quantization decides whether a model fits

Weight memory, against the 16 GB of a Colab T4

  • 7B: all three fit. FP16, 14 GB, leaves almost no room for anything else.
  • 32B: only INT4 comes close, and it takes the whole GPU: in practice it does not fit.
  • 70B: none fits. Even INT4 needs more than twice the GPU.
  • Past some size, quantization is the only way to run a model on given hardware; past a larger size, it is no longer enough.

How model quality depends on the bits per weight

  • Qwen2.5-1.5B, rounded with absmax, the method of the next slides, on the text of the course notes. The methods later in this topic keep more quality at 4 bits

Absmax quantization

Why simple rounding does not work

From weights to integer codes

  • Each weight is replaced by the code of its strip. Stored: the small integers, plus one number to get back. This is why it is lossy: inside a strip, weights can no longer be told apart

Absmax quantization

  • Six real weights and INT4: the codes run to 7. With INT8 they run to 127, and the steps are 18 times finer. Zero stays exactly zero

Exercise 2 · Quantize and dequantize

Complete absmax_quantize and absmax_dequantize. qmax is the largest code of a signed bits-bit integer: 127 for 8 bits. alpha is the largest absolute value. The scale maps alpha to qmax. The cell should print the codes 64, 36, −89, 0, −22, −54, 127, 36, −23.

Hints
  • A signed bits-bit integer goes up to 2bits − 1 − 1.
  • np.abs(x) gives absolute values; np.max the largest one.
  • scale = qmax / alpha. Quantize: multiply by the scale, then round. Dequantize: divide by it.

Exercise 3 · Sweep the bit widths

Choosing the range

Lopsided values: asymmetric quantization

An illustration, with values from −1 to 3

  • Both rows use INT4 codes. Weights center on 0, and absmax suits them; one-sided values do not, like the outputs of ReLU, a common step inside networks that replaces every negative value with 0

One large weight stretches the range for all

  • The blue shape: how many weights land at each x × s. On the real model, with one s for all its weights and 4 bits, 99.99% of the weights get the code 0

Clipping: a narrower range

  • Real weights, INT4, one scale for all the weights. Error: the root mean squared difference between each weight and its stored value

Choosing the range is called calibration

  • Clipping trades one large error for many small ones
  • Calibration: choosing the range, often on a sample of real inputs

Exercise 4 · Asymmetric quantization

Complete asymmetric_quantize and asymmetric_dequantize. The codes run from 0 to 2bits − 1. The scale is the width of the range divided by the number of steps: the step between two codes, so values are divided by it (absmax's scale was the inverse, codes per unit).   • Dequantize: subtract the zero point, then multiply by the scale.

Methods used in practice

GPTQ: round one weight, then correct the others

One output of a learned table: the dot product of two weights with the input x = (2, 1); weights rounded to steps of 0.25

  • The goal: keep the output (the dot product) close to the original
  • GPTQ rounds the weights one at a time; after each, it adjusts the weights not yet rounded to make up for the error
  • The adjustment is computed on a small set of real inputs, the calibration data
  • 4 bits per weight with little loss, without retraining

Frantar et al., “GPTQ: Accurate post-training quantization for generative pre-trained transformers,” ICLR 2023

Post-training quantization (PTQ)

  • Train in full precision, quantize at the end
  • Cheap: minutes to hours
  • The GPTQ checkpoint used here is PTQ

Quantization-aware training (QAT)

  • The forward pass already sees the rounding error
  • Usually more accurate at low precision
  • Expensive: it needs training

BitNet: weights of −1, 0 or +1

A dot product of inputs and weights, with ordinary weights and with ternary weights (three possible values); inputs (2, 5, 3)

  • × +1: keep the input; × −1: flip its sign; × 0: skip it. No multiplication left, only additions.
  • Trained this way from the start, as in QAT
  • One weight is coarse; thousands of them together still give a fine range of sums

Ma et al., “The era of 1-bit LLMs: all large language models are in 1.58 bits,” 2024

What measurements show

Two recent studies, on very different hardware

Shi & Ding (2025): 11 quantization methods, models of 7B to 70B parameters, A100 and H100 GPUsenergy efficiency up to 30% better (34B models)

code generation (HumanEval): share of generated programs that pass their tests, down by up to 92% for the 13B model; smaller models lose more quality

The hardware has to support the format

Peak throughput of the NVIDIA T4, the GPU in Colab, from its datasheet

FP328.1 TFLOPS
FP1665 TFLOPS
INT8130 TOPS
INT4260 TOPS

TFLOPS, TOPS: trillions of floating-point or integer operations per second

  • The T4 has special circuits, tensor cores, for 16-, 8- and 4-bit numbers
  • These are peak numbers: real runs are slower, and memory is often the limit
  • A format the hardware or the software cannot use directly is unpacked first: smaller, but not faster
Discussion · 10–15 minutes

Is quantization a free lunch?

When would quantization not be worth it? Think of:

  • hardware or software that cannot compute in the lower precision
  • too few bits for the task: code, arithmetic, rare words
  • a small model, already close to its quality limit
  • calibration data unlike the real inputs
  • a quality check too weak to notice what broke
