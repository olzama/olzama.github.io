To understand what quantization does, we first need to see how a computer stores a number.

Step 1 · Numbers in bits

A computer stores every number as bits

Binary: the same place values as decimal, with powers of 2

  • A bit is 0 or 1
  • Each position is worth twice the one to its right: 1, 2, 4, 8, …
  • A model's parameters are numbers, so they are bits too: billions of them

A language model is a very large collection of numbers, its parameters. In a computer, every number is stored as a sequence of bits. A bit is 0 or 1.

Binary numbers work like decimal numbers. In decimal, each position is worth ten times the one to its right: ones, tens, hundreds. In binary, each position is worth twice the one to its right: 1, 2, 4, 8.

Let's read the binary number 1101. The board writes the place values above the digits: 8, 4, 2, 1. Then we multiply each digit by its place value. 1 times 8, plus 1 times 4, plus 0 times 2, plus 1 times 1. That is 8 plus 4 plus 0 plus 1, which is 13.

Whole numbers like this are called integers. But most of a model's numbers are not whole numbers. They are numbers like 0.37 or minus 1.85. For those, the computer uses a format called floating point.

A floating-point number has three parts

The IEEE 754 standard: sign, exponent, mantissa

value = sign × (1 + mantissa) × 2exponent − bias

  • FP16: a 16-bit floating-point number. FP32 uses 32 bits.
  • The bias (15 in FP16) lets the exponent be negative as well, for very small numbers

Here is how a floating-point number is stored. This is the FP16 format: a floating-point number in 16 bits. FP32 uses 32 bits in the same way.

The bits are split into three fields, and each field has a different job. The first bit is the sign: plus or minus. The next five bits are the exponent. The exponent sets the scale of the number: it multiplies the number by 2 to some power. The last ten bits are the mantissa, also called the fraction. The mantissa gives the detail inside that scale.

The formula combines the three: the sign, times one plus the mantissa, times 2 to the power of the exponent minus the bias.

What is the bias? The exponent bits are read as an ordinary positive number. But we also need negative exponents, for very small numbers. So the format subtracts a fixed number, the bias. In FP16 the bias is 15. Five bits can store 0 to 31, so after subtracting 15, the exponent can go from minus 14 to plus 15. The values 0 and 31 are kept for special cases, like zero and infinity.

The next screen decodes one real bit pattern, step by step.

Decoding one FP16 number

Here are 16 bits. Let's decode them, one field at a time.

The sign bit is 0, so the number is positive.

The exponent bits are 0 1 1 1 1. We read them as a binary number, as on the first screen. 0 times 16, plus 1 times 8, plus 1 times 4, plus 1 times 2, plus 1 times 1. That is 15. Then we subtract the bias, 15. 15 minus 15 is 0. So the scale is 2 to the power 0, which is 1: no scaling up or down.

The mantissa bits are 1 followed by nine zeros. The mantissa is read as fractions after an implied leading 1. The leading 1 is not stored. The first mantissa bit is worth one half, the next one quarter, the next one eighth, and so on. Only the first bit is 1. So the mantissa is 1 plus one half, which is 1.5.

Now we combine the three fields: plus 1, times 1.5, times 2 to the power 0. The number is 1.5.

It is the same place-value idea as before, used twice: once for the exponent bits, with 16, 8, 4, 2, 1, and once for the mantissa bits, with one half, one quarter, and so on.

The next number after 1 is 1 + 2−mantissa bits: 1 + 2−23, 1 + 2−10, 1 + 2−7.

  • Range: how large or small a number can be. It comes from the exponent bits.
  • Precision: how close two stored numbers can be. It comes from the mantissa bits.
  • FP16 cuts both. A value above 65,504 becomes infinity.
  • BF16 (“brain floating point”, from Google Brain) keeps FP32's 8 exponent bits: the same range, less precision. It is often safer for training.

More bits give two different things.

The exponent bits give the range: how large, or how small, a number can be. FP32 has 8 exponent bits. Its largest number is about 3.4 times 10 to the 38. FP16 has only 5 exponent bits. Its largest number is 65,504. Anything larger becomes infinity, and the computation breaks.

The mantissa bits give the precision: how close two stored numbers can be. Look at the last column: the next number the format can store after 1. FP32 has 23 mantissa bits, so after 1 comes 1.00000012. FP16 has 10 mantissa bits, so after 1 comes 1.00098. Nothing in between can be stored.

BF16 is a third 16-bit format. BF stands for brain floating point. It was introduced by Google Brain, a team at Google. BF16 keeps the 8 exponent bits of FP32, so it has the same range. It has only 7 mantissa bits, so it is less precise: after 1 comes 1.0078. It loses detail, but it avoids the overflow problems of FP16. That is why it is often used for training.

Now the memory. Our model has 1.5 billion parameters. Each parameter is one number. In Session 1, each number used 16 bits.

The board multiplies. 1.5 times 10 to the 9 parameters, times 16 bits per parameter. The parameters cancel, and we get 24 times 10 to the 9 bits. A byte is 8 bits, so we multiply by one byte per 8 bits. The bits cancel: 3 times 10 to the 9 bytes. That is 3 gigabytes.

The bars on the right repeat this for other formats. With 32 bits per number, the weights take 6 gigabytes. With 16 bits, 3. With 8-bit integers, called INT8, 1.5. With 4-bit integers, INT4, 0.75 gigabytes. Each halving of the bits halves the memory.

This is only the memory for the weights. Running the model also needs memory for the intermediate values it computes, and for the software. But the weights are usually the largest part, and they decide whether a model fits on a GPU at all.

Step 3 · Absmax quantization

Quantization: many values, fewer codes

  • Map each 16-bit float to a small integer, a code: INT8 has 256 codes, INT4 has 16
  • Store the codes, plus one number to get back: the scale
  • Lossy: values that are close can get the same code, and then they cannot be told apart

Now quantization itself. A 16-bit float can be one of many thousands of values. Quantization maps each one to a small integer, which we call a code. An 8-bit integer, INT8, has 256 possible codes. A 4-bit integer, INT4, has only 16.

We store the codes, and one extra number per group of values, the scale, to get approximately back to the original values.

The mapping is lossy. Look at the picture. Ten values on the top line map to six codes on the bottom line. Values that are close to each other can get the same code. After that, they cannot be told apart.

The question for the whole session is whether the model still behaves well enough for the task when its numbers are this coarse.

Absmax quantization, worked out

The largest absolute value α gets the largest code, 127; zero stays zero

Example values from Grootendorst, “A visual guide to quantization,” 2024

The simplest method is called absmax, short for absolute maximum. It is symmetric: the range goes from minus alpha to plus alpha, centered on zero.

Here are nine values. First we find alpha, the largest absolute value. That is 10.8. Alpha is not learned or estimated. We simply look through the numbers and take the largest one, ignoring the sign.

Then the scale. In INT8 the largest positive code is 127. The scale s is 127 divided by alpha: 127 divided by 10.8, which is about 11.76. So alpha, 10.8, will become exactly 127.

Now take one value, 3.08. To quantize it, we multiply by the scale: 3.08 times 11.76 is 36.22. Then we round to the nearest integer: 36. The code 36 is what we store.

To get a number back, we divide the code by the same scale: 36 divided by 11.76 is about 3.06. This is called dequantization. The difference, 3.08 minus 3.06, is 0.02. That is the quantization error for this value.

Now the value 3.02. 3.02 times 11.76 is 35.52. It also rounds to 36. So 3.08 and 3.02 now have the same code. After quantization, there is no way to tell them apart.

That is all of absmax: compute one scale, multiply, round. Dequantize by dividing. All the difficult parts of quantization are about choosing the range well and keeping the error small.

Step 4 · Choosing the range

Lopsided values: asymmetric quantization

Map the smallest value β to code 0 and the largest α to the last code; z is the code for 0.0

symmetricasymmetric
range−α … αβ … α
storedcodes, scalecodes, scale, zero point
suitsvalues around 0, like most weightsone-sided values, like the outputs of ReLU

ReLU, a common step inside networks, replaces every negative value with 0.

Absmax is symmetric: the range goes from minus alpha to plus alpha. That suits values that are spread around zero. Most weight matrices are like that.

Some values are one-sided. For example, a common step inside neural networks, called ReLU, replaces every negative value with zero. Its outputs are never negative. With a symmetric range, half the codes would be kept for negative values that never occur.

Asymmetric quantization maps the actual smallest value, beta, to code 0, and the largest value, alpha, to the last code. With 8 bits, that is 255. Because zero is no longer in the middle, we need one more number: the zero point, z. It is the code that stands for 0.0.

The board works through an example: values from minus 1 to 3. The scale is the width of the range, 3 minus minus 1, which is 4, divided by 255 steps. That is about 0.0157. The zero point is the code for 0: 0 minus minus 1, divided by 0.0157, which is 63.69. Rounded, 64. So 0.0 is stored as code 64.

To quantize a value, divide it by the scale and add the zero point. 3 divided by 0.0157 is 191.08. Plus 64 is 255.08, which rounds to 255, the last code. Minus 1 divided by 0.0157 is minus 63.69. Plus 64 is 0.31, which rounds to code 0.

The table on the right compares the two methods. Symmetric is simpler, and zero stays exactly zero. Asymmetric uses the codes better for one-sided values, at the cost of one more stored number.

One outlier stretches the range for everyone

24 ordinary values between −2 and 2, and one value of 256

Absmax has one weak point. The largest absolute value sets the range for every value.

Take 24 ordinary values between minus 2 and 2, and one outlier, 256. Absmax sets alpha to 256. The scale is 127 divided by 256, about 0.496.

Now quantize ordinary values. 1.98 times 0.496 is 0.98, which rounds to 1. 0.5 times 0.496 is 0.25, which rounds to 0. Minus 1.36 times 0.496 is minus 0.67, which rounds to minus 1. All 24 ordinary values get one of only three codes: minus 1, 0 or 1. The outlier is stored well, but the ordinary values lose almost all their detail.

The fix is called clipping. We choose a smaller range, here minus 2.5 to 2.5, and replace anything outside it by the edge of the range. Now the scale is 127 divided by 2.5, which is 50.8. 0.5 times 50.8 is 25.4, which rounds to 25. The ordinary values now use many codes. The outlier is stored as 2.5, an error of 253.5.

This happens in real neural networks. Large language models have a few values that are much larger than the rest, so practical methods have to deal with them.

Clipping is a deliberate trade. We store the outlier badly so that the many ordinary values are stored well. Choosing the range is called calibration. It is often done on a small sample of real inputs.

The table on the left lists three common rules. Percentile: keep all values except the rarest extremes, for example except the top 0.1 percent. Mean squared error: choose the range that makes the average squared error smallest. KL divergence: choose the range that keeps the shape of the distribution of values closest to the original.

There is one more distinction: weights and activations. Weights are the model's parameters. After training they are fixed. They do not depend on the input. So we can quantize them once, carefully, and save the result.

Activations are the intermediate values the model computes while it processes an input. Change the input, and the activations change. So their range is not known in advance.

Quantization applied after training is called post-training quantization, or PTQ. For activations there are two strategies, in the table on the right. Dynamic quantization computes the scale and zero point during each run, from the values it actually sees. It adapts to every input, but it costs extra work. Static quantization fixes them in advance, from calibration data. It is faster, but it only works well if the calibration data looks like the real inputs.

Step 5 · Methods used in practice

GPTQ: round one weight, then correct the others

A toy layer with two weights and the input x = (2, 1); weights rounded to steps of 0.25

  • The goal: keep the layer's output close to the original
  • GPTQ rounds the weights one at a time; after each, it adjusts the weights not yet rounded to make up for the error
  • The adjustment is computed on a small set of real inputs, the calibration data
  • 4 bits per weight with little loss, without retraining

Frantar et al., “GPTQ: Accurate post-training quantization for generative pre-trained transformers,” ICLR 2023

Absmax rounds every weight on its own. With 8 bits that is usually fine. With 4 bits the errors are large, and they add up. GPTQ is a method that makes 4 bits work much better. Today's notebook loads a model quantized with GPTQ.

The idea is that what matters is the output of the layer. The board shows a toy layer with two weights. It computes y equals w1 times x1 plus w2 times x2. The input is 2 and 1. The weights are 0.37 and 0.52. We can only store weights in steps of 0.25.

First the original output: 0.37 times 2 plus 0.52 times 1. That is 0.74 plus 0.52, which is 1.26.

Now round each weight on its own. 0.37 becomes 0.25, and 0.52 becomes 0.5. The output is 0.25 times 2 plus 0.5 times 1. That is 0.5 plus 0.5, which is 1.0. The error is 0.26.

Now the GPTQ way. Round the first weight: 0.37 becomes 0.25. The output loses 0.12 times 2, which is 0.24. Before rounding the second weight, we add that loss to it: 0.52 plus 0.24 is 0.76. Now round it: 0.75. The output is 0.25 times 2 plus 0.75 times 1. That is 0.5 plus 0.75, which is 1.25. The error is only 0.01.

A real layer has thousands of weights and sees many different inputs. GPTQ computes the corrections from a small set of real inputs, called calibration data, so that the output stays close on average. It does this layer by layer. The result is a model with 4 bits per weight, without any retraining. We will not run the calibration ourselves today. We load a checkpoint that the Qwen team already quantized with GPTQ.

GGUF: one scale per block of weights

The file format of llama.cpp, for running models on laptops and CPUs

  • One scale for a whole matrix: its largest weights set the range for all the others
  • One scale per small block: each block gets a range that fits it
  • The cost: each block stores its scale too

Type Q4_0: blocks of 32 weights, 4 bits each, one 16-bit scale

The second method you will meet often is GGUF. GGUF is the file format of llama.cpp, a program for running language models on ordinary computers: laptops, and machines without a large GPU. Many models are shared in this format.

The idea to remember is block-wise quantization. With absmax over a whole matrix, the largest weights set the range for every weight, as with the outlier. Small weights then round to 0. GGUF splits the weights into small blocks, and each block gets its own scale. Each block's range fits its own weights.

The cost is that every block stores its scale. The board works this out for the GGUF type called Q4_0. A block has 32 weights of 4 bits: 32 times 4 is 128 bits. The scale is a 16-bit number. 128 plus 16 is 144 bits. 144 divided by 32 weights is 4.5 bits per weight.

Post-training quantization (PTQ)

  • Train in full precision, quantize at the end
  • Cheap: minutes to hours
  • Today's GPTQ checkpoint is PTQ

Quantization-aware training (QAT)

  • The forward pass already sees the rounding error
  • Usually more accurate at low precision
  • Expensive: it needs training

Everything so far was post-training quantization: the model is trained in full precision, and quantized afterwards. That is cheap: it takes minutes or hours.

Quantization-aware training, or QAT, puts the rounding into training. Look at the loop. The model keeps a full-precision copy of each weight. In every training step, the weight is quantized and dequantized, so the forward pass uses the rounded value and sees the rounding error. The loss is computed, and the update goes to the full-precision weight. Over many steps, the model learns weights that still work after rounding.

QAT is usually more accurate at low precision. But it needs training, or at least fine-tuning, so it costs much more than PTQ. It is not something we can do in a Colab session.

BitNet: weights of −1, 0 or +1

A neuron's weighted sum, with ordinary weights and with ternary weights; inputs (2, 5, 3)

  • × +1: keep the input; × −1: flip its sign; × 0: skip it. No multiplication left, only additions.
  • Trained this way from the start, as in QAT
  • One weight is coarse; thousands of them together still give a fine range of sums

Ma et al., “The era of 1-bit LLMs: all large language models are in 1.58 bits,” 2024

BitNet is at the extreme end. Its weights can only be minus 1, 0 or plus 1. Three values need about 1.58 bits, which is why the paper speaks of 1.58-bit models.

Recall how a neuron works. It takes several input numbers, multiplies each by its own weight, and adds the results. This is a weighted sum. The board shows it for the inputs 2, 5 and 3.

With ordinary weights, 0.4, minus 0.1 and 0.9: 0.4 times 2 is 0.8. Minus 0.1 times 5 is minus 0.5. 0.9 times 3 is 2.7. The sum is 0.8 minus 0.5 plus 2.7, which is 3.0.

With BitNet weights, plus 1, minus 1 and 0: plus 1 times 2 is 2. Minus 1 times 5 is minus 5. 0 times 3 is 0. The sum is minus 3. Look at what happened: there was no real multiplication. Times plus 1 keeps the input. Times minus 1 flips its sign. Times 0 skips it. Only additions are left, and in hardware an addition costs much less energy than a multiplication.

Can such coarse weights work? One weight is very coarse. But a layer has thousands of weights, and the sum of thousands of them can still take very many different values. BitNet models are trained this way from the start, like QAT. You cannot take an ordinary trained model and force its weights to minus 1, 0 and 1: it would break. This is a research direction. Today we run GPTQ.

Here is a map of the methods we saw, from cheapest to most expensive.

Absmax and the zero point are the basic building blocks. On their own, they work well at 8 bits.

Dynamic and static post-training quantization are two ways to handle activations: compute the range in every run, or fix it in advance from calibration data.

GPTQ makes 4 bits work on a GPU without retraining. GGUF formats are for running models on laptops and CPUs. QAT gives the best accuracy at low precision, if you can afford training. BitNet is a different kind of model, trained from scratch for ternary weights.

None of these is best in general. The choice depends on the hardware, the task, how much accuracy you can lose, and what you can afford to compute.

What do measurements show? Here are two recent studies, on very different hardware.

The first, by Shi and Ding, from 2025, tested 11 quantization methods on models from 7 to 70 billion parameters, on large data-center GPUs. Energy efficiency improved by up to 30 percent. The waiting time for the slowest responses went down by up to 70 percent. But quality can break. On a code-generation benchmark, the pass rate of the 13 billion parameter model dropped by up to 92 percent. And they found that smaller models lose more quality than larger ones. Our model is small, so we should not assume that its accuracy stays the same.

The second, by Husom and colleagues, also from 2025, tested 28 quantized models on a Raspberry Pi, a very small computer. The 3- and 4-bit models used up to 79 percent less energy than the 16-bit ones. But fewer bits did not always mean less energy. For Qwen 2.5 with 1.5 billion parameters, the model family we use, every 3-bit version used more energy per token than the 4-bit versions.

Note the words "up to". These are the best cases. The savings depend on the model, the task, the method and the hardware. That is why we measure our own.

TFLOPS, TOPS: trillions of floating-point or integer operations per second

  • The T4 has special circuits, tensor cores, for 16-, 8- and 4-bit numbers
  • These are peak numbers: real runs are slower, and memory is often the limit
  • A format the hardware or the software cannot use directly is unpacked first: smaller, but not faster

Quantization saves memory. Can it also make the computation faster? That depends on the hardware.

Here are the peak numbers for the NVIDIA T4, the GPU you get in Colab, from NVIDIA's datasheet. In 32-bit floating point it does 8.1 trillion operations per second. In 16-bit, 65 trillion. In 8-bit integers, 130 trillion. In 4-bit integers, 260 trillion. The T4 has special circuits, called tensor cores, for these lower precisions.

These are peak numbers. A real run is slower: often the limit is how fast the weights can be read from memory, not how fast they can be multiplied. Smaller weights help with that too.

But the hardware and the software have to support the format. If they cannot compute with 4-bit numbers directly, the numbers are first unpacked to 16 bits, and that costs time and energy. Then the model is smaller, but not faster. So quantization results from one machine do not always transfer to another.

Discussion · 10–15 minutes

Is quantization a free lunch?

When would quantization not be worth it? Think of:

  • hardware or software that cannot compute in the lower precision
  • too few bits for the task: code, arithmetic, rare words
  • a small model, already close to its quality limit
  • calibration data unlike the real inputs
  • a quality check too weak to notice what broke

Discuss with your neighbor, then with the room.

Before the notebook, a short discussion, about ten to fifteen minutes. The question is: when is quantization not worth it?

Here are some directions. The hardware or the software may not compute in the lower precision, so nothing gets faster. Too few bits may break tasks that need exact details, like code or arithmetic. A small model may already be close to its quality limit. The calibration data may be unlike the real inputs. And the quality check may be too weak to notice what broke.

For our own experiment, keep two of these in mind. Our model is small. And our scoring rule is simple: it only checks if the answer contains a keyword.

To close, what we did today.

A number is stored as bits, and fewer bits mean less memory. Quantization maps numbers to a few integer codes: compute a scale, multiply, round, store the codes. The rounding error is the price. How large it is depends on the range: outliers stretch it, clipping and block scales fix it, and the zero point handles one-sided values.
