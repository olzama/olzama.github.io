Where the costs come from

A language model uses electricity, hardware and water for its whole life

TrainingComputing the model's parameters from text. Many processors for days or weeks. Paid once per model.
InferenceRunning the trained model on each request. Small per request, paid every time, for as long as the model is in use.
StorageThe model's parameters and the data, kept on disks and in memory, powered all the time.
Cooling and waterProcessors turn electricity into heat. Data centers remove it, often by evaporating water.
  • Building the hardware has a cost too, before any of this runs.

How do we measure it?

Computation, power and energy

An example: one hour on the GPU you will use in Colab

  • FLOPs (floating-point operations): how much computation. They count the work, whatever the hardware.
  • Power, in watts (W): how fast a device uses electricity, at a given moment.
  • Energy, in watt-hours (Wh) or kilowatt-hours (kWh): power × time. This is what you pay for.

An NVIDIA T4 GPU

A real run draws less than the maximum, so 0.07 kWh is an upper bound for the GPU alone.

From energy to carbon and water

The same hour on the T4, with example values

  • PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness): the data center's total energy ÷ the computers' energy. 1.0 would mean no overhead.
  • Carbon intensity: grams of CO₂eq per kWh of electricity. It depends on where and when: the grid's mix of coal, gas, wind, sun.
  • CO₂eq: all greenhouse gases, converted to the amount of CO₂ with the same effect.
  • WUE (Water Usage Effectiveness): liters of water per kWh used by the computers.
  • Operational carbon comes from running the hardware; embodied carbon, from building it.

Example values. Real ones depend on the data center and the grid.

Step 1 · N-grams

N-grams

What is a language model?

  • It predicts the next word.
  • It gives a probability to every word in the vocabulary. The probabilities add up to 1.
  • An n-gram model is the simplest kind: it counts word sequences in a corpus, then divides.
Credit: Jurafsky & Martin, Speech and Language Processing

After “my”: one probability per word

Counted from three sentences, on the next screen. The vocabulary has 7 words.

N-grams

Count, then divide

  • This is a bigram model: the next word depends on the one word before it.
  • Counting, then dividing: that is the whole model.
  • A pair it never counted gets probability 0. Aunt and uncle appear in the same places, but the model keeps separate counts for each word: what it learns about aunt does not help with uncle.
  • A Galician model has far fewer sentences to count from than a Spanish or English one, so more of its pairs are never counted.
QuestionIs a bigger n better?More context helps in principle. But a longer word sequence is rarer, so the model has seen fewer of them, and more of its counts are 0.

Step 4 · Attention

Attention

Attention was born in machine translation

  • The bottleneck: the whole sentence squeezed into one vector
  • Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio (2015): for every word it writes, the decoder looks back at every source word and computes how much to use each one: the attention weights (scores, then softmax, as for p)
  • Computed anew for every word written, through learned tables that backpropagation trains (diagram, right; next screen: query, key and value)
  • Which source word goes with which word written (alignment) comes out of the attention weights, with no hand-written rule
  • Invented for translation, years before today's LLMs

Attention weights: Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio, ICLR 2015 (arXiv:1409.0473), Figure 3(a), read from the paper and redrawn

Attention

Query, key and value

  • Query q, computed from the decoder's state: what the decoder looks for, before the word it is about to write
  • Key k: what each source word offers to be found by
  • Score = q · k, the dot product; softmax turns the scores into attention weights that add up to 1
  • Value v: what each source word contributes; the mix is the sum of the values, each times its attention weight
  • On the attention page (next screen), the keys and the values are the same vectors: the encoder's, after each word

Like a search in a library. You come with a question: the query. Every book has a label on its spine: its key. You compare your question with each label, and the better the match, the more of that book you read: the attention weight. What you take away is a blend of the books' contents, the values, mostly from the best matches. The decoder does this before every word it writes, each time with a new question.

Step 5 · Self-attention

Self-attention

Self-attention: every word looks at every word of its own sentence

  • Vaswani et al. (2017): the same mechanism within one sentence: every word looks at every word, itself included, all at once
  • “it” → “the animal”, with no hand-written rule
  • No decoder here: every word's vector gives its own query, key and value, each through its own learned table (attention page, section 6, “one head”)

Step 6 · Transformers

Transformers

A transformer

  • Orange: information moves between tokens. Green: each token on its own
Transformers

Sequential and parallel computation

The problem both layers solve: the output for a token must hold information from the other tokens

  • Recurrent: the output for one token is an input for the next, as in the encoder of Step 3: this is how earlier tokens reach later ones
  • Transformer: no waiting, but the comparisons grow with the square of the length (Step 5), the recurrent steps only with the length. The comparisons are independent, so a GPU runs them side by side; the time grows once they exceed what it can run at once
Transformers

GPUs and matrix multiplication

  • The query table: a matrix of parameters set by training, the same for every token. It turns each token's vector into its query (attention page, section 6); in papers, a weight matrix
  • Small number in a cell: the core that computes it. Cores are many and simple, and all do the same operation on different numbers (the T4 of your notebooks has 2,560)
  • Our sentence, 35 tokens of 512 dimensions: one token's row is 512 sums, all the rows 17,920

Transformers

What a decoder may look at

Encoder (BERT): every token looks at every token

Decoder (GPT): only at itself and the tokens before it

  • Rows look, columns are looked at, as in the grids you read. ×: the weight is forced to 0
  • The decoder's job is to write the next word: if it could see that word, there would be nothing to learn
Transformers

From two stacks to one

  • The only thing removed is the separate encoder stack; its work moves into the single stack
  • Translation has an input and an output, but both are text: they can be one sequence, and the same layers can build the vectors of both
Transformers

Training on raw text

  • The task of Step 1, the next word, now with all the text before it. Every position of every text gives a gold output, with nobody writing one
  • Why this teaches so much: predicting well takes grammar, meaning and facts, and translation too, because text contains it: PaLM's training data held over 30 million translation pairs

Numbers: GPT-2 (124 million parameters), trained only on this task. Source: Briakou et al., “Searching for Needles in a Haystack,” ACL 2023 (arXiv:2305.10266)

Why does model size cost so much?

What doubling each one does to the computation of one use

double…and the computation
the parameters (wider vectors, more blocks)doubles for every word read or written
