Here is the plan for the technical part of today. We go in six steps. Each step fixes a problem of the step before.

Step 1 is n-grams. An n-gram model predicts the next word by counting which words follow which. Its problem: it has no idea that aunt and uncle are similar. They are just two different words.

Step 2 is embeddings. Each word gets a vector, a list of values, learned from the words around it. Similar words get similar vectors. But these are vectors for single words. They say nothing about a sentence.

Step 3 is the encoder–decoder model for translation. The encoder reads the whole sentence into one vector. The decoder writes the translation from that vector. Its problem: one vector has to hold the whole sentence, and the early words fade.

Step 4 is attention. The decoder may look back at every source word, and it decides how much to use each one. But the source sentence is still read one word after another.

Step 5 is self-attention. Every word looks at every other word of its own sentence, all at the same time.

Step 6 is the transformer: many blocks of self-attention stacked on top of each other. This is the architecture of today's large language models. Its cost grows with the size of the model and the length of the text. That is where the rest of the course starts.

Step 1 · N-grams

What is a language model?

  • It predicts the next word.
  • It gives a probability to every word in the vocabulary. The probabilities add up to 1.
  • An n-gram model is the simplest kind: it counts word sequences in a corpus, then divides.

After “my”: one probability per word

Counted from three sentences, on the next screen. The vocabulary has 7 words.

We start with the statistical era of NLP, and with one idea: the language model.

A language model predicts the next word. It does not make one guess. It gives a probability to every word in the vocabulary. The probabilities add up to 1. Most words get a very small probability.

The chart shows this for the word “my”. Aunt gets 0.67. Uncle gets 0.33. Every other word gets 0. These numbers come from three sentences. We count them on the next screen.

This same task is behind everything from the n-gram models of the 1990s to GPT. An n-gram model is the simplest version. It does not learn anything in the neural sense. It counts how often word sequences occur, and it divides.

Step 1 · N-grams

Count, then divide

The corpus

my aunt stayed quietmy aunt spoke wiselymy uncle stayed quiet
P(wi | wi−1) = Count(wi−1 wi)Count(wi−1)
P(aunt | my) = 2/3 ≈ 0.67P(uncle | my) = 1/3 ≈ 0.33
  • This is a bigram model: the next word depends on the one word before it.
  • Counting, then dividing: that is the whole model.
  • A Galician model has far fewer sentences to count from than a Spanish or English one, so its estimates are noisier.
QuestionIs a bigger n better?More context helps in principle. But a longer word sequence is rarer, so the model has seen fewer of them, and more of its counts are 0.

An n-gram is a sequence of n words in a row. A bigram is 2 words. A trigram is 3. This is a bigram model. It predicts the next word from the one word before it.

Our corpus has three sentences about an aunt and an uncle. The formula asks: of all the times “my” appeared, how often was the next word “aunt”? “My” appears 3 times. “My aunt” appears 2 times. So the probability of aunt after my is 2 out of 3, about 0.67. “My uncle” appears once, so uncle gets 1 out of 3, about 0.33.

Counting, then dividing. That is the whole model. There is no hidden step.

Ask the class: is a bigger n better? Let them answer. Then press Show one answer. A longer n gives more context. But the exact longer sequence is rarer in any corpus. So more of the counts are 0. This is called sparsity.

Galician has much less text to count from than Spanish or English. So a Galician n-gram model has more zero counts and noisier numbers. Every count in this toy example stands for that bigger problem.

Step 1 → Step 2

What n-grams cannot see

my aunt stayed quietmy aunt spoke wiselymy uncle stayed quiet
P(spoke | aunt) = 1/2P(spoke | uncle) = 0
  • Aunt and uncle appear in the same places. To the model they are two unrelated rows of counts.
  • What it learns about aunt does not help with uncle.
  • Step 2, embeddings: give each word a vector, so that words used in similar ways get similar vectors.

Step 2 continues on the slides, from “From counts to vectors” (slide 28).

Here is what step 1 cannot do. In our corpus, aunt and uncle are used in the same way. Both follow “my”. Both are followed by “stayed”.

The corpus has “aunt spoke”, but never “uncle spoke”. So the model gives “spoke” after uncle a probability of 0. It does not know that aunt and uncle are similar words. For the model, they are just two different rows in a table of counts.

Step 2 fixes this. Each word gets a vector, learned from the words around it. Words used in similar ways get similar vectors. Then what the model learns about aunt can help with uncle. We continue on the slides, at slide 28.
